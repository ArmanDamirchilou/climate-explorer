Test the method with answers we know.

We generated records with known slopes, then checked whether each method recovered them. This tests statistical behavior under specified conditions. These records are synthetic. They are never presented as NASA observations.

What failed, and why it matters

Years can share a persistent pattern of error. That makes an ordinary interval too confident. A dependence adjustment can help, but short records still cause problems. Our experiment did not find a deployable interval method that passed every predeclared screening case.

Agreement between several methods does not prove a trend. They share data and may share weaknesses. Our application shows conditional comparisons rather than claiming that one adjustment solves every case.

Errors are Gaussian with marginal variance one. In the AR(1) scenarios, each year's error depends on the previous error by the stated correlation. Other scenarios increase the noise over time or add a single extreme endpoint. A known-covariance oracle is included as a control. It has information we do not have for a real location.

The automatic Newey-West lag is floor(4(n/100)^(2/9)). All Newey-West intervals use Bartlett weights, the n/(n−2) correction, and Student t critical values. Theil-Sen uses its ordinary interval in this experiment, which does not correct dependence. GLSAR estimates an AR(1) process with up to ten iterations.

What this does not establish

The simulation does not cover every climate process. It does not validate source data, resolve changing observation networks, test all nonlinear trends, or correct repeated exploration of locations and dates. Rates here are not measured false-positive rates for Tehran. No real user benefit has yet been measured.
